JOB MARKET PAPER

When the Mean Is Not Enough:

The Distribution of Inflation Expectations and Inflation Dynamics

I study whether average inflation expectations capture the information that matters for inflation dynamics. Using U.S. household survey data from 1990–2025, I find that changes in the distribution of expectations contain information beyond the average: during the COVID inflation surge, the right tail thickened before the broader distribution shifted upward.

Show abstractHide abstract

The representative-agent New Keynesian Phillips Curve assumes common inflation expectations across agents, while empirical applications typically summarize heterogeneous expectations by their mean. This paper asks whether the mean is a sufficient statistic for the role of expectations in inflation dynamics or whether the distribution of expectations also matters. Using individual responses from the University of Michigan Survey of Consumers from 1990 to 2025, I estimate inflation-expectation densities and use functional principal component analysis to characterize changes in their shape over time. During the COVID inflation surge, the right tail of the distribution thickened before the entire distribution shifted rightward, suggesting that changes in the distribution can provide early signals of inflationary pressures. The results suggest that the mean is not a sufficient statistic; inflation dynamics also depend on how inflation expectations are distributed across households.

From Regional to Aggregate: Trade Openness and the Phillips Curve Break After 2000

Show abstractHide abstract

This paper studies how trade openness reshaped the slope of the U.S. Phillips Curve after 2000. I develop an open-economy New Keynesian framework in which inflation dynamics depend on domestic slack and exposure to foreign-goods prices through trade openness. Empirically, I use U.S. regional panel data and exploit cross-regional variation in exposure to globalization using a shift-share instrument constructed from pre-2000 regional employment shares. The results suggest that trade openness contributed to a break in the Phillips Curve relationship after 2000.
