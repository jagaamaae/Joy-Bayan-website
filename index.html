JOB MARKET PAPER

Is the Mean Enough?

The Distribution of Inflation Expectations and the Aggregate Phillips Curve

The representative-agent New Keynesian Phillips Curve summarizes inflation expectations by their mean, reducing the cross-sectional distribution of beliefs to a single point forecast. This paper examines whether the mean is a sufficient statistic for inflation dynamics. Using individual responses from the University of Michigan Survey of Consumers from 1990 to 2025, I compare the mean expectation with distributional features summarized by dispersion, directional shifts, and tail risk. Accounting for omitted distributional features—particularly dispersion and tail risk in the full sample—produces a steeper aggregate Phillips Curve while the mean expectation becomes insignificant. During COVID, changes in the distribution begin in the tails before developing into a rightward shift, whereas during the Great Recession a leftward shift occurs before the lower tail expands. Hence, the distribution contains information beyond its mean.

When the Poor Pay More for Groceries: Online Retail Transformation and Monetary Policy

With Xudong An (Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia) and Rodney Ramcharan (University of Southern California)

Using 100 million grocery retail transactions from 228,519 U.S. households between 2019 and 2023, we show that low-income households experienced 1.4% more grocery price inflation than high-income households in the post-COVID period. After 2022, fresh-produce prices rose 5.3% more for low-income households. The increased adoption of online shopping eliminated geographic pricing advantages, reducing relative savings for low-income households by 4.4%, while the expansion of warehouse clubs increased their price premiums by 6.4%, with monetary policy tightening adding a further 0.11%.

From Regional to Aggregate: Trade Openness and the Phillips Curve Break After 2000

This paper examines whether trade openness can explain the break in the U.S. Phillips curve after 2000. Within an open-economy New Keynesian framework, I use U.S. regional panel data and exploit China’s accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001 as a source of trade-induced structural change. I construct a shift-share instrument using pre-2000 regional employment exposure to tradable industries, and find that greater trade exposure contributed to the flattening of the U.S. Phillips curve.
